I took roughly forty thousand swings in college for about four hundred at-bats
that anyone watched. That ratio never struck me as unfair — it's just what the
work is. Then I started writing software professionally and noticed the industry
mostly talks about at-bats.

## Boring practice beats interesting practice

The drills that made me better were the least fun ones. Tee work. Same swing,
same spot, endlessly. The dev equivalent isn't learning a new framework every
weekend; it's reading the codebase you already work in, tracing a request all
the way through, and writing the small unglamorous test.

Things that have compounded for me:

- Reading a diff line by line before approving it
- Reproducing a bug before theorizing about it
- Writing the commit message first, to find out if I actually understand the change

## Getting hit is information

Kickboxing has a clarifying quality: your model of the situation gets corrected
immediately and physically. You can't argue with a body shot. Code review, on-call
pages, and user testing are the same mechanism with better lighting — feedback
that shows up whether or not you were ready for it.

The skill isn't avoiding it. It's shortening the gap between being wrong and
knowing you're wrong.

## Slumps are a process problem

Every hitter's instinct in a slump is to change everything. The good coaches make
you change one thing and measure. I do this now when a project stalls: pick the
single smallest verifiable step, ship it, see what moved. Rewriting the whole
module because I'm frustrated is the tech equivalent of a new batting stance in
game four of a slump.

## What I actually took with me

Nobody claps for the reps. They still decide the at-bat.
