The word "user" is doing a lot of quiet damage. It flattens a frightened
daughter coordinating her dad's discharge and a nurse on hour eleven of a
twelve-hour shift into the same abstract shape with the same abstract needs.
Working on CareMobi cured me of it fast.

## The form nobody could finish

Our onboarding flow was, on paper, excellent. Eleven fields, clean validation,
inline errors, a progress bar. It tested beautifully. Then we watched real
families use it and the completion rate cratered around field six — the one
asking for a diagnosis.

Turns out people filling out a care profile often don't know the diagnosis yet.
That's frequently _why_ they're there. We'd built a form that required you to
already have the thing you were looking for.

The fix wasn't technical:

1. Make every clinical field optional
2. Ask for a relationship instead ("I'm caring for my father")
3. Let the profile fill in over weeks, not minutes

Completion went up sharply, and support tickets that started with "I don't know
what to put" disappeared.

## Latency is a trust problem

In most apps a 900ms response is a polish issue. In a care app, a spinner
between "send" and "your nurse got this" is someone wondering whether their
message about medication reached a human. We moved to optimistic updates with
an explicit delivery state, and stopped treating the loading state as a
throwaway:

```jsx
<Message status={message.status} /> // queued | sent | seen
```

Three words of state removed an entire category of anxious follow-up messages.

## Accessibility isn't an edge case here

Our median caregiver was not a twenty-eight-year-old with perfect vision on a
new iPhone. Font scaling, contrast, and tap targets weren't a compliance
checklist — they were the product working or not working. Once I started
building with that as the default assumption, the "accessible" version just
became the version.

## The part that stuck

I now try to name the person before I write the component. Not `user`, not
`account` — "Marguerite, 71, on her daughter's iPad, tired." It sounds twee.
It also catches more design mistakes than any code review I've ever sat in.
