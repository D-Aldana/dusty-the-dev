Most faith apps want your attention. Streaks, badges, a 7 a.m. push notification
telling you that you're falling behind on a reading plan you never chose. I
wanted the opposite: a quiet room. You type what you're carrying, you get one
verse back, and then the app gets out of your way.

## The first version was too generous

My first pass did what every side project does — it grew. Journals, history,
saved verses, a share sheet, a streak counter I told myself was "gentle."
Two weeks in I opened it on a bad night and felt the same low-grade pressure I
was trying to design away.

So I deleted most of it:

- No account until you actually need one
- No notifications at all, not even opt-in
- No history for the first release
- One verse per session, and you can't reroll it

That last one was the argument I had with myself the longest. Letting people
reroll would have made the app feel more responsive and less like a slot machine
you only get one pull on. But a verse you can reject fifty times isn't an answer
anymore — it's a search result.

## Where the AI actually sits

Claude doesn't write scripture and it doesn't paraphrase it. It reads the
entry, picks a theme, and selects from a curated passage set. The verse text
itself is never generated:

```ts
const theme = await classifyEntry(entry) // grief, fear, gratitude, ...
const verse = pickVerse(theme, { excludeRecent: session.seen })
```

Keeping generation out of the sacred text was non-negotiable, and it made the
whole feature easier to reason about. A classifier that's occasionally wrong
gives you a slightly off verse. A generator that's occasionally wrong invents
scripture.

## What I'd tell myself at the start

Restraint is a feature you have to build, not one you get by skipping work. Every
thing Manna doesn't do took a deliberate decision, a rejected PR, or a
half-finished screen I deleted. The app is small because I kept choosing small.

Next up: I'm slowly adding a private, on-device history — because the one thing
people asked for was the ability to look back at what they wrote, and that turns
out to be the rare feature that doesn't cost anyone their peace.
