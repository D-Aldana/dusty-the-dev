Being laid off surprised me. The dread that followed did not.

My first thought was not profound. It was that I had to enter the job market again.

Then came the financial calculations. The savings plan I had made for a future home. The travel I had hoped to do. The question of how much of that future I was willing to spend while searching for whatever came next.

Beyond the immediate practical stress, the layoff interrupted my sense of momentum. After more than two years of professional experience, I had started to feel as though I was building a stable foundation for my career and my life.

That is the part I keep returning to. Not the circumstances of the layoff, but the question it left behind. I am experienced enough to know what shipping real software involves, but early enough in my career that an interruption can still make me reconsider where I fit entirely.

## Two years is an awkward amount of experience

Over the past two years, I have worked across web and mobile applications, often in healthcare. On one project, I built most of a caregiver-facing web dashboard myself.

I primarily owned the frontend, but the boundaries were never rigid. I contributed to the backend and deployment, spoke directly with clients, and had room to make product decisions and suggest better approaches. The work mattered to people navigating healthcare, and I could see how my decisions affected the usefulness of the product.

That experience taught me that I am good at solving problems. It also taught me that the work I enjoy most is not simply completing tickets. I want to understand why something should exist, influence how it works, and feel responsible for whether it helps anyone.

But two years is an awkward amount of experience in the current market. I am no longer brand new, yet many roles ask for a depth of experience I have not had time to accumulate. Job listings attract enormous numbers of applicants, and getting a resume in front of a real person can feel like a separate skill from being able to do the job.

At the same time, the skills used to evaluate developers are changing.

I spent years learning how to turn requirements into code. Now someone with little or no coding experience can use AI to produce a convincing MVP in an afternoon. That does not make the result reliable, maintainable, or ready to scale. It does change what is scarce.

Code is no longer the hardest thing to produce.

Understanding and validating what was produced might be.

## When the developer starts directing the work

AI has made me much faster. I use agents throughout my development process, including implementation, review, and iteration.

I usually begin by laying out the architecture I want and defining the constraints I can see. I ask the agent to push back where my instructions are unclear or where I may have missed something. From there, I delegate the initial implementation and review, then inspect and test the result myself. We repeat that loop until I am satisfied with what we have built.

In that workflow, I often feel less like the person laying every brick and more like the architect responsible for the building.

That can be empowering. I can explore an idea and move from concept to working software faster than I could have imagined when I started my degree. It can also be disorienting. I spent a long time developing skills that no longer feel valuable in quite the same way.

But the architect metaphor has a limit. Architects do not develop sound judgment simply by standing farther away from the construction. Their decisions are informed by an understanding of materials, constraints, and all the ways a design can fail. Software is no different. It is difficult to evaluate an implementation if you have never struggled through one yourself.

If an agent can produce the code, where exactly do I fit?

The easy answer is that developers simply need to “adapt to AI.” I agree, but I do not find that answer especially useful. Adapt how? Which skills deserve years of deliberate practice when the tools themselves may look completely different next year? How do you build confidence early in your career when the ground beneath that confidence keeps shifting?

There is a contradiction here that I am still working through. Early-career developers have traditionally built judgment by doing the implementation work. We try an approach, discover where it fails, debug our assumptions, and try something else. If an agent takes over much of that work, it might also remove some of the experiences that teach us how to evaluate its output.

I do not think the solution is to avoid AI or insist on writing everything manually. The important part is preserving the experiment. AI can shorten the distance between an idea and a result, which means I can test more approaches in less time. But speed only becomes experience if I stay involved in the loop.

I try to understand exactly what the agent is doing and how its solution could be improved. That means inspecting the result, testing the assumptions behind it, and questioning decisions I do not understand. If I cannot explain how the important parts of the system work, then a successful output is not enough.

Maybe judgment has never come from laying the bricks alone. It comes from seeing what happens when you arrange them one way, learning why the structure fails, and trying again. AI changes how quickly that cycle happens, but it does not remove my responsibility to learn from it.

My current answer is that the differentiator is not whether you can get software to appear. It is whether you understand the system that appeared.

Can you identify its faults? Can you see where it will fail under real use? Do you know what happens when the happy path ends, the data grows, the requirements conflict, or the product needs to scale? Can you recognize when the agent solved the wrong problem elegantly?

Most importantly, are you willing and able to take responsibility for the result?

AI can participate in a review, but it cannot own the consequences of shipping. It can generate possible architectures, but it does not understand a client's needs in the way that comes from listening to them, asking uncomfortable questions, and noticing what they have left unsaid. Communication, judgment, and accountability are not side skills around the “real” engineering work. Increasingly, they are the work.

## The job search is a human problem too

The same lesson applies to finding a job.

When a market is saturated, the tempting response is volume: send more applications, tailor more resumes, and hope that one makes it through the filters. I understand that approach because I feel the pressure behind it. Still, my first real software job did not come from being application number 347 in a queue.

It came from a connection.

I had applied to the company years earlier while I was still in school. That earlier interaction became a relationship, and that relationship later turned into an opportunity. Someone knew more about me than a collection of keywords could communicate.

That experience changed how I think about networking. It is not collecting strangers so that I can ask them for a referral. It is giving people a chance to understand how I think, what I care about, and what I might contribute before a job opening forces the conversation.

That is part of why I have been working on my own projects, improving my website, becoming more active on LinkedIn, and writing this article as I prepare for what comes next. I cannot control which companies are hiring or whether an application reaches a human being. I can make my work and thinking more visible. I can start conversations. I can keep building.

## I am still looking for where I fit

I would like to end with a confident prediction about the future of software engineering. I do not have one.

I still worry that AI could replace more of what I currently consider valuable, including parts of problem-solving itself. I am still trying to decide which skills will matter most and how an early-career developer can demonstrate them. I am still facing a job search I did not expect, while reconsidering plans I thought were becoming secure.

But uncertainty is not the same as having nothing to offer.

I know what it feels like to take meaningful ownership of a product. I know how to move between frontend, backend, deployment, client conversations, and product decisions when the work requires it. I know how to use AI to extend my abilities without confusing generated output for understanding. And I know that I care whether the thing I build makes a difference. I do not want to add another button that nobody will use.

Maybe finding my place in software is no longer about choosing a fixed position on a familiar career ladder. Maybe it is about becoming the kind of person a team can trust to understand a problem, direct powerful tools, communicate clearly, and own an outcome.

I have not found my place yet. But I am getting clearer about what I want it to be.
